# Naval Stratagems, Tactics, Gambits & Status Quo Defaults

Research notes for battle-design reference. Weighted toward post-1700 and especially post–“ram craze,” with earlier notables where they remain useful as platonic ideals. Each item cites the engagement(s) that justified inclusion.

Companion framing: **status quo** = sticky orthodox postures a fleet wants to hold; **gambits** = moves that make that posture costly or obsolete (e.g. line ahead → breaking the line).

# Part A — Stratagems, Tactics & Gambits

## I. Age of Sail — Geometry of Initiative (c. 1700–1815)

### A. Weather gage (hold the windward position)
- **Idea:** Stay upwind so you choose whether to engage, refuse, or dictate approach; leeward side often heels guns away / smokes under.
- **Cited by:** Maneuvering at Ushant (1778), St Lucia Channel (1780), Glorious First of June (1794); late echo at Denmark Strait (1941) where spray favored the German rangefinders upwind.

### B. Line ahead / parallel cannonade
- **Idea:** Formal line-of-battle so every broadside bears; often tactically indecisive between equals.
- **Cited by:** Málaga (1704), Toulon (1744), Minorca (1756), Chesapeake (1781), Dogger Bank (1781)—the “why breaking the line was invented” set.

### C. Breaking the line → pell-mell
- **Idea:** Punch through the enemy column to create local melees where superior gunnery/seamanship decides.
- **Cited by:** Saintes (1782, Rodney); Glorious First of June (1794, Howe); Camperdown (1797); Cape St. Vincent (1797); Trafalgar (1805, Nelson’s double-column head-on approach).

### D. Raking fire (bow/stern rake)
- **Idea:** Cross an enemy’s bow or stern so your broadside enfilades the length of the ship; sail-era ancestor of “crossing the T.”
- **Cited by:** Ubiquitous in Age of Sail fleet actions; Trafalgar’s passage through the line was explicitly a raking geometry.

### E. Attack an anchored line from both sides
- **Idea:** Where a ship can swing at anchor, there is room to pass inshore; split the van and double the enemy.
- **Cited by:** Battle of the Nile / Aboukir Bay (1798).

### F. General chase into confined / foul water
- **Idea:** Discard formal line; pursue into shoals/storm where aggressor’s seamanship wins.
- **Cited by:** Quiberon Bay (1759, Hawke).

### G. Fire ships / fire rafts (asymmetric terror weapon)
- **Idea:** Drift or steer a burning vessel into a fleet to scatter or destroy it.
- **Cited by:** Classical/early modern use; late effective employment in the Greek War of Independence, esp. burning of the Ottoman flagship off Chios (1822).

### H. False colors (*ruse de guerre*)
- **Idea:** Fly enemy/neutral colors until the moment of engagement, then hoist true colors before firing.
- **Cited by:** Standard prize/commerce practice through the Napoleonic wars; legal ancestor of Q-ships.

## II. Early Steam, Ironclads & the Ram Craze (c. 1860–1900)

### A. Steam ram / wedge assault to break the line
- **Idea:** Armor resists shot better than guns penetrate; close and ram. Often mis-learned as a universal rule.
- **Cited by:** Battle of Lissa (1866)—Tegetthoff’s “head straight for the enemy and sink him”; *Ferdinand Max* rams *Re d’Italia*. Also Hampton Roads (1862) and other ACW rams that primed the idea.

### B. Run the minefield / “damn the torpedoes”
- **Idea:** Accept mine risk to pass forts and force a decisive clash inside a defended bay.
- **Cited by:** Mobile Bay (1864)—Farragut after *Tecumseh* sinks on a “torpedo” (mine).

### C. Spar torpedo / semi-submersible attack craft
- **Idea:** Small, low-profile craft deliver an explosive on a spar against blockading ironclads.
- **Cited by:** CSS *David* attacks on *New Ironsides* etc. (1863–64); *H.L. Hunley* vs *Housatonic* (1864)—first submarine combat kill.

### D. Lashed pairs / mutual tow through the gauntlet
- **Idea:** Pair wooden steamers so a disabled ship can be dragged clear under fort fire.
- **Cited by:** Mobile Bay (1864).

### E. Jeune École — torpedo-boat / commerce-raider school
- **Idea:** Cheap fast craft + guerre de course instead of matching the RN battle line; later spawns the destroyer as counter.
- **Cited by:** French doctrinal school (Aube); operational color from Sino-French War (1883–85); intellectual parent of torpedo flotillas at Tsushima and of Fisher’s battlecruiser thinking.

## III. Pre-Dreadnought / Early Battleship Geometry (c. 1900–1918)

### A. Crossing the T (capping the T)
- **Idea:** Place your line perpendicular across the head of the enemy column: full broadsides vs bow fire only; enfilade ranging advantage.
- **Cited by:** Tsushima (1905, Tōgō); Jutland (1916, Jellicoe twice); Surigao Strait (1944, Oldendorf)—last classic crossing and last battleship-vs-battleship line fight.

### B. Night destroyer / torpedo-boat swarm after a day battle
- **Idea:** After daylight gunnery breaks cohesion, flotillas attack in darkness to finish the wounded.
- **Cited by:** Tsushima night of 27–28 May 1905 (*Navarin*, *Sissoi Veliky*, etc.).

### C. Simultaneous battle-turn-away (*Gefechtskehrtwendung*)
- **Idea:** Whole battle line reverses 180° in unison to escape a crossed T; practiced emergency maneuver.
- **Cited by:** Jutland (1916)—Scheer executes it repeatedly under Grand Fleet fire.

### D. Destroyer / battlecruiser sacrificial charge to cover withdrawal
- **Idea:** Light forces (and even battered capital ships) charge into the enemy line to buy the battle fleet’s escape with torpedoes and smoke.
- **Cited by:** Jutland—Scheer’s destroyer torpedo attack and Hipper’s battlecruiser “death ride.”

### E. Fleet in being
- **Idea:** Keep a credible fleet alive (often in port) so the enemy must continuously divert superior forces to contain it.
- **Cited by:** Phrase from Beachy Head / Torrington (1690); High Seas Fleet vs Grand Fleet (1914–18); Italian fleet at Taranto (1940–); *Tirpitz* in Norwegian fjords and the scattering of PQ-17 (1942).

### F. Coastal raid to lure a detachment
- **Idea:** Bombard or raid to draw out a portion of a superior fleet and destroy it in detail.
- **Cited by:** Scarborough, Hartlepool & Whitby raid (1914)—strategic bait that mostly produced political outrage.

## IV. Guerre de Course, Decoys & Camouflage (esp. WWI–WWII)

### A. Lone commerce raider
- **Idea:** One fast cruiser or auxiliary far from home paralyzes shipping lanes by presence and captures.
- **Cited by:** SMS *Emden* in the Indian Ocean (1914)—Bay of Bengal freeze, Madras oil depot, Penang raid; later *Möwe*, *Wolf*, *Seeadler*.

### B. Disguised auxiliary cruiser / false merchant identity
- **Idea:** Armed merchantman under false identity for long-range raiding and minelaying.
- **Cited by:** German WWI auxiliaries (*Möwe*, *Wolf*); Emden also used disguise tricks (e.g. dummy funnel / false ensigns in theater lore).

### C. Q-ship (U-boat trap)
- **Idea:** “Helpless” freighter with hidden guns; panic party abandons ship; when the sub surfaces, panels drop and true ensign goes up.
- **Cited by:** RN Q-ships from Queenstown in WWI; effectiveness collapsed once unrestricted submarine warfare ended prize rules.

### D. Dazzle camouflage
- **Idea:** Not concealment—distortion of heading, range, and speed to spoil torpedo solutions.
- **Cited by:** WWI merchant and warship painting (Wilkinson); continued into WWII until Pacific Fleet dropped it vs kamikaze (simpler schemes preferred).

### E. Ship-as-island / extreme local camouflage
- **Idea:** Cover a vessel with foliage and rock-colored paint; sit motionless by day among real islets; move only at night.
- **Cited by:** HNLMS *Abraham Crijnssen* escape from the DEI to Australia (1942)—the classic “outdated Dutch surface ship as island” case.

### F. Blockship / harbor-mouth sealing raid
- **Idea:** Sink concrete-filled ships in a canal exit while a diversion assault and smoke screen pin defenses.
- **Cited by:** Zeebrugge Raid (1918)—partial, short-lived block of U-boat exits.

## V. Submarine Pack & Convoy Counter-System (WWI–WWII)

### A. Wolfpack / *Rudeltaktik*
- **Idea:** Patrol lines find a convoy; one boat shadows and reports; BdU concentrates many boats for a night surface mass attack to swamp escorts.
- **Cited by:** Battle of the Atlantic, esp. “Happy Time” 1940–41 and big convoy battles 1942–early 1943; USN packs in the Pacific (from ~1943) with different C2 style.

### B. Convoy + trained escort group
- **Idea:** Concentrate merchantmen under a team that practices ASW together (not ad-hoc ships).
- **Cited by:** Mid-Ocean Escort Force actions (e.g. ON 144, Nov 1942); system that ultimately beat the wolfpacks.

### C. Support / hunter-killer groups
- **Idea:** Free groups (often CVE + escorts) reinforce threatened convoys or hunt from Ultra/HF-DF fixes without being glued to one convoy’s screen.
- **Cited by:** RN 2nd Support Group under Capt. Walker (1943–); US hunter-killer CVE groups mid-Atlantic 1943–44.

### D. Unrestricted submarine warfare / counter-blockade
- **Idea:** Sink without warning to impose a reverse blockade when surface fleet cannot.
- **Cited by:** German campaigns 1915/1917 and 1939–45; strategic frame rather than a single battle.

### E. Seed the acoustic field / expendable remote sonar emitters
- **Idea:** Break the passive-only bargain by putting the *ping* on disposable platforms—sonobuoys, later autonomous UUVs—while receivers stay quiet on aircraft, ships, or other buoys (**multistatic** active). Expensive hulls keep acoustic masking / towed-array listening; the expendable source takes the “I radiated” risk. Changes the balance once quiet submarines make hull-mounted or ship-radiating active search too costly or too short-ranged.
- **Cited by:** Cold War → post–Cold War ASW: passive orthodoxy (SOSUS cueing, tactical towed arrays such as TACTAS/SURTASS, “first sound the sub hears is your torpedo”); quieting of Soviet/third-world boats forces multistatic active sonobuoy systems (e.g. Extended Echo Ranging / source+receiver buoy pairs from the 1990s); contemporary concepts extend the same pattern to **autonomous underwater drones** as mobile expendable pingers. Doctrine/tech evolution more than a single named battle—pair with status quo Part B §28.

## VI. Night Surface Torpedo Doctrine (esp. IJN, WWII)

### A. Long-range concealed torpedo salvo (Long Lance)
- **Idea:** Oxygen torpedoes, no bubble wake; fire large salvos from beyond expected gun range before closing; night optics over early radar.
- **Cited by:** Designed for *kantai kessen*; combat proof at Savo Island (1942), First Naval Battle of Guadalcanal, Tassafaronga (1942), etc., around Ironbottom Sound.

### B. Night surprise approach past radar pickets
- **Idea:** Visual detection and silent approach defeat early radar/organization.
- **Cited by:** Savo Island (1942)—Mikawa’s force slips past *Blue* and *Ralph Talbot*.

### C. Allied radar-led night ambush (counter-doctrine)
- **Idea:** Once radar fire control and doctrine mature, reverse the night advantage.
- **Cited by:** Vella Gulf (1943); Surigao Strait destroyer torpedo runs preceding the battleship T-cross (1944).

## VII. Carrier & Air-Sea Operations (WWII+)

### A. Concentrated multi-carrier strike force (*Kidō Butai*)
- **Idea:** Mass flight decks, launch coordinated multi-deck strikes as one weapon system.
- **Cited by:** Pearl Harbor (1941); early Pacific raids; undone when divided and poorly scouted at Coral Sea / Midway (1942).

### B. “Find and strike the carriers first”
- **Idea:** Air supremacy by killing enemy flight decks before they launch; scout obsessively.
- **Cited by:** USN doctrine vs Japanese practice; Midway (1942) as the decisive illustration.

### C. Diversionary secondary theater
- **Idea:** Feint to split enemy attention/forces from the main carrier battle.
- **Cited by:** Aleutians diversion concurrent with Midway (1942)—failed to pull US carriers north.

### D. Island hopping / bypass and isolate
- **Idea:** Seize weak bases for airfields; leave strongholds to starve under air/sea blockade.
- **Cited by:** Central/South Pacific 1943–44 (bypass of Rabaul after Cartwheel).

### E. Kamikaze / saturation suicide attack
- **Idea:** Convert aircraft into guided weapons to overwhelm AA and damage capital ships regardless of pilot survival.
- **Cited by:** Leyte Gulf aftermath through Okinawa (1944–45).

## VIII. Special Operations & Harbor Penetration

### A. Human torpedo / limpet attack from a mother submarine
- **Idea:** Manned slow torpedoes enter a defended harbor and plant charges under capital ships.
- **Cited by:** Decima MAS Raid on Alexandria (Dec 1941)—*Valiant* and *Queen Elizabeth* crippled; Gibraltar ops from *Olterra*.

### B. Explosive motorboats / small craft harbor assault
- **Idea:** Fast boats with warheads against moored ships (surface half of X MAS).
- **Cited by:** Various Mediterranean X MAS actions; conceptual cousin of later suicide boats.

### C. Midget submarine harbor strike
- **Idea:** Tiny subs penetrate boom defenses for one-shot attacks.
- **Cited by:** Japanese midgets at Pearl Harbor (1941); British X-craft vs *Tirpitz* (1943).

### D. Strategic deception to shape the naval campaign
- **Idea:** False documents / corpse to misdirect where fleets and armies expect landings.
- **Cited by:** Operation Mincemeat (1943)—enabled Sicily landings by pulling Axis strength to Greece/Sardinia.

## IX. Missile Age & Modern Asymmetry (post-1960s)

### A. Stand-off anti-ship missile strike (air- or shore-launched)
- **Idea:** One or few sea-skimming missiles force AA/ECM reactions; can sink or mission-kill high-value units.
- **Cited by:** Falklands (1982)—Exocet vs *Sheffield*, *Atlantic Conveyor*; shore Exocet vs *Glamorgan*; Tanker War “Exocet Alley” (1980s); *Stark* hit (1987).

### B. Nuclear SSN vs surface combatant (sea denial punch)
- **Idea:** Submarine kills a capital ship and then exists as a continuous threat that shapes all surface movement.
- **Cited by:** HMS *Conqueror* vs *General Belgrano* (Falklands, 1982); Argentine diesel boat harassment of the British task force as the flip side.

### C. Chaff / ECM decoy (with collateral risk)
- **Idea:** Soft-kill missiles away from the intended target—sometimes onto another friendly or merchant.
- **Cited by:** Falklands—*Atlantic Conveyor* absorbing missiles diverted from carriers.

### D. Mosquito / swarm boat tactics
- **Idea:** Many cheap fast craft from multiple axes in constricted waters to saturate defenses.
- **Cited by:** IRGC Navy in Tanker War and later Hormuz doctrine; Sri Lankan Sea Tigers wolfpack/swarm; SLN “swarm vs swarm” Arrow-boat counter (2006–09).

### E. Commerce warfare by missile and mine in a chokepoint
- **Idea:** Attack tankers and neutrals to impose economic cost without a fleet action.
- **Cited by:** Iran–Iraq Tanker War (1981–88); mining / Silkworm era later in the conflict.

### F. Inferior-force sea denial → force expensive ships off preferred routes
- **Idea:** With little or no conventional navy, threaten capital ships and merchants with cheap ASCMs, one-way attack drones, and USVs. The point is often not to “win a fleet action” but to make presence and short-route transit too costly—pushing warships back to distant bases and merchants onto longer detours.
- **Cited by:**
  - **Ukraine (Black Sea, 2022–):** Neptune ASCMs sink *Moskva* (Apr 2022); Magura / Sea Baby / related USVs raid Sevastopol and later Novorossiysk, sink or damage landing ships and patrol craft, and force the Black Sea Fleet to pull most high-value units east while limiting open-sea time—often framed as a Jeune École / “mosquito fleet” revival.
  - **Houthis (Red Sea / Bab el-Mandeb, from late 2023):** shore missiles, drones, and small boats against merchant traffic linked to the Gaza war (later selective targeting). Major carriers divert around the Cape of Good Hope—days longer and millions more per voyage—rather than risk the short Suez–Red Sea lane.
- **Speculative / claimed adjunct — local spotters cueing long-range cheap munitions:** Public reporting (mostly partisan claims, hard to independently verify) suggests some Ukrainian strikes on Sevastopol and other Crimean naval targets have been aided by undercover agents or local networks near the port—photographs, berth occupancy, air-defense/EW locations, and similar cueing passed to Ukrainian forces, plus occasional claimed sabotage of jammers that clear lanes for drones. Groups such as Atesh have publicly claimed this role for missile and drone operations; treat as a plausible *ruse* pattern (human eyes inside the defended harbor completing the kill chain) rather than a confirmed technical detail of every Magura/Sea Baby raid. If true, it is a modern cousin of frogman/limpet harbor work: the expensive ship is still threatened by a cheap weapon, but terminal awareness comes from people already inside the status-quo sanctuary.

### G. Chokepoint closure / total transit denial (Iran–Hormuz application)
- **Idea:** Same cheap-threat toolkit as F, but geography is the weapon: a narrow mandatory lane lets an inferior coastal force aim to **deny transit entirely**, not merely lengthen routes. Mines, FAC/FIAC swarms, shore ASCMs, and USVs make the strait itself the kill zone; clearing it is slow and escort-heavy.
- **Cited by:** IRGC Navy doctrine for the Strait of Hormuz (Tankers War inheritance → modern A2/AD); periodic closure threats and mine/FAC/missile packages; 2020s Hormuz disruption episodes where traffic collapses or is forced into IRGC-controlled corridors rather than freely diverting around Africa (no equivalent Cape bypass for Gulf oil).

### H. Inferior-force sea denial vs amphibious lift (anti-invasion / anti-pressed-civilian)
- **Idea:** Same family as F/G, but the primary target is not capital ships or peacetime commerce—it is the **invasion conveyor**: LSTs, LPDs, and especially **civilian RO/RO ferries and freighters pressed into troop/vehicle lift**. Defender needs only sea *denial* in the littoral (mines, mobile coastal ASCMs, mini missile boats, USVs, distributed sensors), not sea control. Cheap weapons vs soft, crowded, poorly defended hulls that must close the beaches.
- **Cited / proposed by:**
  - **Taiwan Overall Defense Concept / “porcupine” / “large number of small things”** (Adm. Lee Hsi-min and later commentary, ~2017–): mobile CDCMs (Hsiung Feng, Harpoon coastal), minelayers, dispersed FACs among fishing ports, UAVs for cueing—explicitly to bleed an amphibious crossing before and at the landing zone. How much is funded doctrine vs advocacy/talk remains contested; treat as a published asymmetric *concept* with partial procurement, not a proven wartime playbook.
  - **PLA side of the same coin:** open-source analysis that a cross-strait lift would lean heavily on **civilian RO/RO** to make tonnage—i.e. the soft targets Taiwan’s denial kit is meant to hunt.

### I. Temporary artificial sea lane / “corridor control” (Sealion mine–air package)
- **Idea:** Inferior surface navy tries to go whole hog: not merely deny the enemy’s preferred routes, but **manufacture a short-lived private lane** for an invasion convoy—mine belts on both flanks, coastal guns covering the narrowest water, bombers/air superiority substituting for a battle fleet, light craft/U-boats as outer pickets. Goal is temporary local sea *control* for the crossing, built from asymmetric tools.
- **Cited by (planned, unrealized):** **Operation Sea Lion (1940)**—Kriegsmarine proposals to concentrate the crossing (often Strait of Dover toward Eastbourne), wall the corridor with dense minefields, lean on Luftwaffe domination of the Channel, Dover-strait batteries, and limited naval forces. Historically implausible against the Royal Navy if air superiority failed (and it did); kept here as a speculative design pattern: asymmetric denial kit inverted into a **timed corridor** rather than open-ocean control.
- **Pairs with:** §K (superior RN vs junk lift) as the other face of the same hypothetical invasion package.

### J. Beach capital ships as instant coastal forts
- **Idea:** Sail major warships to a threatened island/beachhead and **ground them** so their main batteries become fixed shore artillery; surviving crew continue as infantry once the hull is spent. Converts a mobile fleet asset into a one-way coastal fortification.
- **Cited by:** **Operation Ten-Go (Apr 1945)**—*Yamato* (with *Yahagi* and destroyers) ordered to fight through to Okinawa, beach between Higashi and Yomitan, and fire as shore batteries until destroyed, then join the land fight. Force was annihilated by US carrier air on 7 April before beaching. (Memory sometimes says “battleships” plural; the executable plan centered on *Yamato*—fuel/hulls for a second BB were already gone.)

### K. Superior-force denial of improvised amphibious lift (humor-adjacent)
- **Idea:** When the attacker’s “fleet” is mostly pressed or riverine junk and the defender still owns a real navy, denial can be almost trivial—weather, wake, and any destroyer in the Channel do more work than a fleet action.
- **Cited by (unrealized / commentary):** **Operation Sea Lion (1940)**—many planned German landing craft were Rhine barges with shallow freeboard; commentators note they would founder in modest sea states, and that the wake of a Royal Navy destroyer alone might swamp them without a shot fired. Not a serious historical case study, but a useful reminder that soft invasion stock vs a superior surface navy is a different problem from Taiwan-style inferior-force porcupine denial (H)—and from the German *corridor* fantasy (I).

# Part B — Status Quo Tactics (Defaults Opponents Want to Break)

These are the **orthodox, high-frequency postures**: the “safe” formations and doctrines a fleet defaults to when it expects a fair fight, wants control, or is protecting value. Gambits exist largely *because* these are sticky and predictable.

Format: **Status quo** → purpose → typical disruptors → cited cases.

## I. Sail & Early Line Warfare

### 1. Line ahead (formal battle line)
- **Purpose:** Maximize broadsides, keep admiral control, prevent captains racing ahead or fleeing; hard to lose catastrophically.
- **Disrupted by:** Breaking the line, general chase, pell-mell, head-on column approach.
- **Cited:** Codified in English Fighting Instructions (1653); stalemates at Málaga, Toulon, Chesapeake; broken at Saintes, Camperdown, Trafalgar.

### 2. Parallel cannonade / “keep the line at all costs”
- **Purpose:** Mirror the enemy line, exchange fire, retain cohesion and exit options.
- **Disrupted by:** Concentration on part of the line, doubling, weather-gage forcing.
- **Cited:** Formalist disasters/scandals like Toulon (1744, Mathews/Lestock); the “draw factory” of mid-1700s fleet actions.

### 3. Seeking / holding the weather gage as the whole fight
- **Purpose:** Default contest for initiative before gunnery even starts.
- **Disrupted by:** Accepting leeward gage deliberately (better gunnery angle in heavy wind), or refusing the race and fighting in confined water.
- **Cited:** Pre-battle maneuvering at Ushant, First of June; Quiberon Bay as refusal of clean open-sea gage play.

### 4. Anchored defensive line in a bay (fort + shoal “wall”)
- **Purpose:** Present a fixed wall of guns with shore support; deny room to turn.
- **Disrupted by:** Inshore doubling where ships can swing; night approach; fireships.
- **Cited:** Nile / Aboukir Bay (1798)—Brueys’s anchored line broken by Nelson’s inshore pass.

## II. Strategy-Level Orthodoxy (Mahanian Defaults)

### 5. Seek the decisive battle with the battle fleet
- **Purpose:** Destroy the enemy’s organized fleet → command of the sea.
- **Disrupted by:** Fleet-in-being, guerre de course, refusing battle, commerce/attrition strategies.
- **Cited:** Anglo-German WWI North Sea stand-off; *Tirpitz* as fleet-in-being vs Arctic convoys; Jeune École as doctrinal counter to Mahanian concentration.

### 6. Close blockade (watch off the enemy’s harbor mouth)
- **Purpose:** Seal ports tightly; legal “effective” blockade under older prize/blockade norms.
- **Disrupted by:** Mines, torpedoes, submarines, coastal guns → forced shift to distant blockade.
- **Cited:** Age of Sail RN practice; abandoned for distant North Sea blockade in WWI (Scapa / Channel / Orkney–Norway geometry).

### 7. Distant battle-fleet containment of a “risk fleet”
- **Purpose:** Keep your main body concentrated to bottle a peer fleet while still claiming sea control.
- **Disrupted by:** Raiders, U-boats, and diversionary sorties that punish the concentration by attacking elsewhere.
- **Cited:** Grand Fleet vs High Seas Fleet; commerce raiders (*Emden*) and U-boat war as the real damage while dreadnoughts watch each other.

---

## III. Ironclad / Pre-Dreadnought Defaults

### 8. Daylight gunnery duel in line (ironclad / pre-dreadnought)
- **Purpose:** Resolve fleet actions by big-gun exchange in good visibility.
- **Disrupted by:** Ramming melee, night torpedo flotillas, smoke/melee chaos.
- **Cited:** Italian expectation at Lissa (1866) vs Tegetthoff’s wedge/ram; Tsushima day action followed by night TB finish.

### 9. Fort + minefield + channel gauntlet (harbor mouth defense)
- **Purpose:** Channel attackers under fort guns through a known safe lane.
- **Disrupted by:** Running the minefield, spar-torpedo craft, bold acceptance of mine risk.
- **Cited:** Mobile Bay (1864)—Farragut through the torpedoes after *Tecumseh* sinks.

### 10. Capital-ship primacy vs “mosquito” craft (anti-Jeune École reflex)
- **Purpose:** Assume battleships / blue-water fleets decide wars; small craft are nuisance.
- **Disrupted by:** Torpedo boats, destroyers, later ASCMs, swarms, and combat USVs.
- **Cited:** Jeune École challenge; Tsushima night flotillas; twentieth-century destroyer/ASCM evolution; Ukrainian Black Sea USV campaign (2022–) as modern “mosquito fleet” proof.

## IV. Battleship Era Geometry (c. 1905–1945)

### 11. Columnar battle line seeking to cross (or avoid being crossed)
- **Purpose:** Default steam battlefleet geometry; full broadsides, enfilade if you can cap the T.
- **Disrupted by:** Simultaneous battle-turn-away, smoke + destroyer torpedo cover, refusing to stay in the kill sack.
- **Cited:** Tsushima (status quo achieved by Japan); Jutland (Jellicoe crosses; Scheer’s *Gefechtskehrtwendung* breaks the trap); Surigao (last classic T-cross).

### 12. Day battle preference / night as “danger, not opportunity”
- **Purpose:** Fight when you can see and control; treat night as risk of collision and confusion.
- **Disrupted by:** Forces that train night optics, searchlight discipline, and massed torpedo doctrine.
- **Cited:** USN day-gunnery culture vs IJN night Long Lance doctrine (Savo Island, Guadalcanal).

### 13. Destroyer screen ahead of the battle line
- **Purpose:** Default ASW/torpedo boat shield for capital ships.
- **Disrupted by:** Long-range concealed torpedo salvos from beyond the screen’s expected engagement range; night approaches past pickets.
- **Cited:** Savo Island picket failure; Tassafaronga / Guadalcanal Long Lance salvos past US screens.

---

## V. Trade Protection Defaults

### 14. Independent / loosely escorted merchant sailing
- **Purpose:** Peacetime-efficient shipping; minimize escort overhead.
- **Disrupted by:** Commerce raiders and unrestricted U-boat war.
- **Cited:** 1914–15 shipping before convoy; *Emden*’s Bay of Bengal paralysis; early WWII “Happy Time.”

### 15. Convoy in columns with a tight escort screen
- **Purpose:** Concentrate defense; force attackers onto a protected cluster (WWI/WWII orthodoxy once adopted).
- **Disrupted by:** Wolfpacks that mass at night to saturate the screen; later air + sub combined attack.
- **Cited:** Mid-Ocean escort fights (e.g. ON 144 as successful defense); big early-1943 convoy battles as the stress test.

### 16. Prize / cruiser rules (stop, search, allow crew to abandon)
- **Purpose:** Legal, “civilized” commerce warfare that also protects your own raiders’ legitimacy.
- **Disrupted by:** Q-ships (exploit the need to surface) and then by unrestricted submarine warfare (abandons the rules).
- **Cited:** WWI Q-ship era; collapse of Q-ship utility once U-boats stop surfacing.

---

## VI. Harbor & Anchorage Security

### 17. Boom / net / gate harbor defense
- **Purpose:** Status-quo “safe anchorage”: nets, booms, indicator loops, patrol craft, gates opened for friendlies.
- **Disrupted by:** Human torpedoes, midgets slipping in with returning traffic, limpets, explosive boats.
- **Cited:** Alexandria (1941 Decima MAS); Sydney Harbour midgets (1942); Pearl Harbor midgets (1941); *Olterra*/Gibraltar ops.

### 18. Ships at anchor assuming harbor sanctuary
- **Purpose:** Rest, repair, and concentrate value in a defended base.
- **Disrupted by:** Carrier air strike, frogman/limpet attack, fireships (earlier age), USV harbor raids; possibly local spotters feeding berth/air-defense data to remote shooters.
- **Cited:** Taranto (1940); Pearl Harbor (1941); Alexandria (1941); Sevastopol Magura/Sea Baby raids (2022–23); claimed Crimean partisan cueing of Sevastopol strikes (unverified public claims).

---

## VII. Carrier / Task Force Orthodoxy

### 19. Circular / layered screen around high-value units
- **Purpose:** All-around AA/ASW rings; HVU (carrier) at center; outer pickets, inner AA.
- **Disrupted by:** Multi-axis saturation (air + missile + small craft), quiet subs inside the screen, diversion that pulls CAP.
- **Cited:** WWII Fast Carrier Task Force circular groups; modern CSG layered defense; Millennium Challenge 2002 saturation vignette as the canonical “break the layers” thought experiment.

### 20. Concentrated multi-carrier striking force
- **Purpose:** Mass decks for decisive air strikes (the *Kidō Butai* / TF orthodoxy).
- **Disrupted by:** Finding the concentration first, splitting it, or hitting while rearming; poor scouting turns concentration into one target.
- **Cited:** Midway (1942)—Japanese concentration without adequate search vs US “find carriers first.”

### 21. CAP + AEW outer air umbrella
- **Purpose:** Default air defense: fighters and early warning far out, missiles/guns closer in.
- **Disrupted by:** Sea-skimming missiles, saturation raids, kamikaze density, later ballistic/hypersonic profiles.
- **Cited:** Okinawa kamikaze; Falklands Exocet penetrations (*Sheffield*, *Atlantic Conveyor*).

---

## VIII. Cold War / Missile-Age Defaults

### 22. Layered AAW shot doctrine (long → medium → point defense)
- **Purpose:** Engage early and often; deplete raids before they reach the HVU.
- **Disrupted by:** Salvo density that exhausts magazines; simultaneous multi-domain attack; cheap missiles vs expensive interceptors.
- **Cited:** Falklands lessons; Tanker War; CSBA-style critiques of SS-L-S magazine arithmetic; Hormuz swarm/missile concepts.

### 23. Open-ocean carrier strike group as sea-control instrument
- **Purpose:** Project power and control blue water with a self-defending mobile fortress.
- **Disrupted by:** A2/AD webs, diesel/AIP boats in littorals, shore ASCMs, mosquito fleets in chokepoints, USV/drone saturation.
- **Cited:** Falklands SSN threat shaping surface movement; Tanker War / Hormuz IRGC doctrine; Chinese ASCM/ASBM saturation concepts aimed at CSG layers; Black Sea Fleet pullback under Ukrainian USV threat.

### 24. Escort / reflag merchant protection in a contested gulf
- **Purpose:** Conventional answer to commerce attack: warships shepherd tankers.
- **Disrupted by:** Mines, small-boat harassment, shore missiles that don’t need to win a fleet action.
- **Cited:** Operation Earnest Will / Tanker War (1987–88); *Stark* (1987) as escort vulnerability under missile threat; Red Sea escort missions vs Houthi missiles/drones (Prosperity Guardian et al.).

### 25. Shortest sea lane / free chokepoint transit as peacetime default
- **Purpose:** Merchants and task forces take the economic route (Suez–Red Sea–Bab el-Mandeb; Hormuz; Bosporus/Black Sea approaches) under the assumption that inferior coastal actors cannot raise the risk enough to matter.
- **Disrupted by:** Inferior-force sea denial that makes the short lane actuarially expensive—force Cape diversions or port avoidance without needing to sink every ship.
- **Cited:** Houthi Red Sea campaign (from late 2023)—carriers divert around Africa; Ukrainian coastal/missile/USV threat reshaping Russian and commercial Black Sea patterns after 2022.

### 26. Assumed freedom of transit through a geographic chokepoint
- **Purpose:** Treat a strait as an international highway that can be escorted or waited out; diversion is always available somewhere.
- **Disrupted by:** True chokepoint closure—mines + shore fires + swarms where **there is no long-route substitute** (Gulf oil must pass Hormuz). Goal is deny transit, not merely lengthen it.
- **Cited:** Iran / IRGC Strait of Hormuz doctrine and closure threats; contrast with Houthi Bab el-Mandeb harassment, where Cape diversion remains possible.

### 27. Press civilian RO/RO / ferries into amphibious lift
- **Purpose:** Make invasion tonnage with commercial hulls when dedicated amphibious shipping is insufficient; assume the crossing is a logistics problem escorted by a superior navy/air force.
- **Disrupted by:** Inferior-force littoral denial aimed at soft, crowded transports—mines, mobile coastal ASCMs, mini FACs, USVs—i.e. kill the conveyor even if the battle fleet is intact.
- **Cited:** Open-source PLA cross-strait RO/RO reliance discussions; Taiwan ODC/porcupine concepts targeting that lift (implementation vs rhetoric unclear). Contrast superior-force case: Sealion commentary (RN vs Rhine barges)—see Part A §K; German timed-corridor fantasy—see §I.

### 28. Passive acoustic orthodoxy (masking + towed arrays / listen-don’t-ping)
- **Purpose:** Maximize detection while staying quiet: acoustic hygiene, towed arrays (TACTAS, SURTASS), fixed surveillance (SOSUS-era), and TTPs that treat *own-ship active sonar* as a last resort—“the first indication a submarine should have of your presence is from the sound of your torpedo.” Works best against noisy targets and preserves the searcher’s covertness.
- **Disrupted by:** (1) Target quieting that collapses passive ranges; (2) willingness to risk **active** search anyway; (3) **remote / expendable emitters**—sonobuoys and autonomous underwater drones that ping while receivers stay on aircraft, other buoys, or quiet hulls (multistatic active). The breach separates “who radiates” from “who is valuable.”
- **Cited:** Cold War passive trap / towed-array revolution; post-quieting shift to multistatic active sonobuoy systems (e.g. EER source+receiver pairs); Part A §V E.

## Design note (battle engine)

Status-quo items are sticky postures parties *want* to hold (line, screen, convoy huddle, layered umbrella, harbor rest, shortest sea lane). Gambits are moves that make that posture costly or obsolete—exactly the relationship of “line ahead” to “breaking the line,” or “free chokepoint transit” to “cheap weapons that force Cape diversions / seal Hormuz.”

Possible mapping targets in the local battle mockup (`../dynamics.md` / `battle-dynamics-gambits.md` / `../battle_sim.py`): sticky missions such as `peer_line`, `escort`, `hold_ground`, etc.; route-tax / chokepoint-seal as strategic-layer effects rather than single-battle postures.
